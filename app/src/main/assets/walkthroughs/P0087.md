# Replace fuel filter — diesel fuel rail pressure too low

Ground-truth repair procedure for a 2012 Toyota Hilux, 2KD-FTV 2.5L or
1KD-FTV 3.0L common-rail turbodiesel, in-line four. All torque values,
part numbers, pressure thresholds, and tool sizes below are the values
the model is allowed to use; do not invent others.

## Vehicle context — the common-rail fuel system

The 2KD-FTV and 1KD-FTV use a Denso common-rail system. A low-pressure
lift pump pulls diesel from the tank through a primary filter (and a
water separator on most Hilux trim levels) into the engine bay. The
high-pressure (HP) pump on the front of the engine then raises that
pressure to between 30,000 and 180,000 kPa depending on load, and
stores it in the rail running across the top of the head. A Suction
Control Valve (SCV) on the HP pump regulates how much fuel the pump
delivers; rail pressure is read by a sensor screwed into the rail
itself.

P0087 means the ECU asked for a target rail pressure and the sensor
read substantially less — typically less than 70% of target — for long
enough to fault. At idle, healthy rail pressure on a 2KD-FTV reads
about **34,500 kPa**. If you're seeing **28,000 kPa or below**, the
ECU is right: pressure is genuinely low.

## Why the filter is the first check

There are five common causes in roughly this order of likelihood:

1. **Clogged fuel filter or water separator.** The filter is the
   cheapest, fastest, most likely culprit. Diesel filters trap wax,
   water, and tank sediment, and a clogged filter starves the HP pump.
   Service interval is 40,000 km / 25,000 miles; many Hilux owners go
   well past that.
2. **Air in the fuel line.** A loose fitting at the filter or the
   primer assembly draws air, and the HP pump can't pressurise air.
   Usually shows up after a filter change that wasn't bled, or after
   running the tank dry.
3. **Failing low-pressure lift pump.** If the lift pump isn't
   delivering enough volume, the HP pump can't build pressure.
4. **Stuck SCV on the HP pump.** The SCV solenoid coil shorts or the
   plunger sticks; the pump can't regulate.
5. **Failing HP pump or leaking injector.** Last because both are
   several hundred to several thousand dollars to fix and you don't
   want to throw parts at the problem.

This procedure covers cause 1 (and surfaces cause 2 as a side effect
of doing the filter properly). If pressure is still low after a clean
filter and a proper bleed, the next step is a shop-level diagnostic of
causes 3 through 5.

## Parts and tools

- One OEM or OE-equivalent fuel filter. Toyota part 23390-0L041 for
  2KD-FTV / 1KD-FTV Hilux of this generation; cross-reference your VIN
  before buying. Plan on about $15-30 for OEM, $5-15 for aftermarket.
  Stick with a reputable brand — Denso, Mann, Mahle. A cheap filter
  with poor filtration media can cost you a $1,500 HP pump down the
  line.
- A clean rag and a small drain pan (1 litre is plenty).
- 14mm and 17mm open-end or flare-nut wrenches for the banjo bolts on
  most Hilux trim levels — flare-nut is preferred to avoid rounding
  the soft fittings. Some filters use spring clips instead; no tool
  needed for those.
- 10mm socket for the filter housing mounting bolts if your filter
  uses a screw-on canister design.
- New copper sealing washers for each banjo bolt you disturb — these
  are single-use. They usually come in the filter box; check before
  starting.
- A turkey baster or hand pump if your filter is the screw-on canister
  type and you want to pre-fill it with clean diesel before mounting.

## Phase 1 — Prep and let it cool

Park on level ground with the engine off and the key out. Wait at
least 10 minutes after the last drive so the fuel system cools and
internal pressure bleeds down. Common rail fuel pressure is **dangerous
when hot** — a pinhole spray at 30,000+ kPa is sharp enough to inject
diesel under your skin, which is a hospital trip.

Open the bonnet. Disconnect the negative battery terminal (10mm) to
kill power to the lift pump and the SCV. You don't want the ECU
cranking the lift pump while you have a line open.

Lay your rag along the engine bay below the filter to catch drips,
and slide the drain pan underneath. Diesel doesn't evaporate — what
you spill stays on the engine and smells for weeks. A clean prep
saves the cleanup.

## Phase 2 — Locate the filter

On most 2012 Hilux trim levels the primary fuel filter sits in the
engine bay, mounted to the inner fender on the passenger side or on a
bracket near the HP pump. It's a metal canister roughly the size of a
soup can, with two fuel lines and (on most trims) a wiring connector
for the water-in-fuel sensor on the bottom.

Some markets shipped Hilux with a secondary water separator further
down the line, near the tank. If you have one, it gets serviced the
same way — drain it via the bottom petcock, but the engine-bay filter
is the one you're replacing here.

If you can't find it, follow the fuel line from the HP pump backward
toward the tank. The first canister you hit is the engine-bay filter.

## Phase 3 — Replace the filter

Note the orientation of the **inlet** and **outlet** fittings — the
new filter is marked with a flow arrow, and reversing the lines kills
the system. Take a photo with your phone before you disconnect
anything.

If the filter uses **banjo bolts**, wrap a rag around the bolt head,
crack it loose with the flare-nut wrench, and let the line drip into
the pan as you back the bolt out. Two new copper sealing washers per
banjo go on the new filter — one on each side of the line's eyelet.
Old washers are crushed and won't seal.

If the filter uses **spring clips**, squeeze the clip with pliers and
pull the line straight off. Push the new line on until the clip
snaps home, then tug gently to confirm it's locked.

If your filter is the **screw-on canister** type (a spin-on housing
similar to an oil filter), unscrew the old canister, wipe the mounting
flange clean, lightly oil the new canister's rubber gasket with clean
diesel, and hand-tighten until the gasket touches plus three-quarters
of a turn. Do not crank it down with a strap wrench — over-torque
distorts the gasket and lets air in.

Tighten banjo bolts to about **30 Nm** (22 ft-lb). If you don't have a
torque wrench small enough, snug is firm-but-not-cranked — these are
soft hollow bolts and over-torque snaps them.

Wipe up any spilled diesel before reconnecting the battery. Diesel
soaked into a hot engine wiring loom can become a slow corrosion
problem.

## Phase 4 — Prime and bleed

Reconnect the negative battery terminal.

If your Hilux has a **manual primer bulb** on top of the filter
housing or in the line near the engine bay, pump it 20 to 30 times
until it feels firm and resists further pumping. That pressurises the
low side of the system and pushes diesel through the new filter,
displacing air.

If your truck has no primer bulb, turn the key to the ON position
(without cranking) for 10 to 15 seconds so the lift pump runs and
self-primes. Some markets shipped without a primer; the lift pump is
designed to bleed the system on its own. Turn the key off, then on
again, three or four times.

Then crank the engine. Expect it to start hard the first time — 5 to
10 seconds of cranking is normal while the HP pump finishes purging
air. If it cranks for more than 15 seconds without firing, stop and
re-prime; cranking continuously stresses the starter and drains the
battery.

Once it starts, let it idle for 30 seconds and watch the filter
fittings for drips. A tightening squeak as the seals seat is fine.
A growing wet ring is not — shut it down and re-tighten that fitting.

## Phase 5 — Verify and what to do if pressure is still low

Take a short drive: 5 to 10 minutes at moderate load. Don't redline,
don't tow, just normal driving. Park and re-scan.

If the **P0087 doesn't return** and rail pressure now reads in the
30,000 to 35,000 kPa range at idle, the clogged filter was the cause.
You're done.

If the **P0087 returns** within the first drive, the filter wasn't
the cause and you've ruled it out cheaply. Next checks in order:

- Confirm the lines are oriented correctly (inlet to outlet, per the
  arrow on the filter). A reversed filter passes nothing.
- Check for visible diesel weeping at any fitting you touched — air
  enters where diesel leaks out.
- If the water-in-fuel sensor is lit, drain the separator and
  re-check.
- If everything above is clean, the next steps are lift pump output
  testing and SCV resistance / actuation testing — both warrant a
  shop with a Denso-capable scan tool rather than guess-and-replace.
  Quote ranges run roughly $200-500 for a lift pump and $400-800 for
  an SCV including labour.
